LGBTQ+ travel

Welcoming, not just tolerant

A certified LGBT Business Enterprise through the NGLCC — and a husband, a shared passport drawer, and a fairly detailed mental list of which resorts hand same-sex couples two twin beds.

The reason to work with an LGBTQ+ advisor isn't solidarity. It's that the research is different, and most people booking online don't know which questions to ask until they're standing at a check-in desk having the wrong conversation.

What I actually check

The bed

It sounds trivial and it is the single most common complaint I hear. A booking that says "one king" arrives at some properties as two singles pushed apart, because a local reservations team made an assumption. It's fixable — but it's fixable in advance, by having the booking noted and reconfirmed, not by having an awkward conversation in a lobby at eleven at night after a nine-hour flight.

The gap between the law and the lobby

These are different things in both directions. There are destinations with excellent legal protections where a particular resort's culture is chilly, and destinations with no legal recognition at all where the tourism sector is entirely relaxed and has been for decades. Neither fact is on a booking site. Some of it I know first-hand, some of it comes from suppliers and other advisors, and where I don't know I'll say so rather than guess with your holiday.

Whether the marketing is backed by anything

A rainbow on a landing page in June costs a resort nothing. What I look for is whether staff are actually trained, whether same-sex couples appear in the property's own photography rather than a stock library, and whether the wedding and celebration programmes are written inclusively or just don't say. Properties that have done the work are usually easy to distinguish from properties that have done the graphic design.

Where this matters most

  • Cruising — ships are generally excellent, and the ports are the variable. An itinerary can be perfect for six days and awkward on the seventh, and it's worth knowing which day that is before you book rather than after.
  • All-inclusive resorts — enormous range. Some properties are genuinely, unremarkably welcoming. Some are fine but will get the room wrong. A few are best avoided, and I'll tell you which.
  • Small group tours — largely about the operator and the guide rather than the destination. Some operators are outstanding here.
  • Honeymoons — where the celebration programme has to be requested properly, which is exactly where assumptions creep in. More on honeymoons →

Family travel

Two-dad and two-mum families hit a specific set of friction points: documentation questions at borders that different-sex parents never get asked, kids' club registration forms with "Mother" and "Father" printed on them, and the occasional supplier system that simply cannot record two parents of the same gender. None of it is insurmountable and all of it is easier if someone has thought about it before you're standing at immigration.

What NGLCC certification means

The National LGBT Chamber of Commerce certifies businesses that are majority-owned and operated by LGBTQ+ people, and the process involves genuine verification rather than a form. Practically, it means two things for you: this is an LGBTQ+-owned business rather than one with an inclusive marketing strategy, and if your employer runs a supplier-diversity programme, bookings through me may qualify — which matters more than you'd expect for group travel and corporate incentive trips.
