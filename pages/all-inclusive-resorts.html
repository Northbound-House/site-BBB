All-inclusive resorts

One price, and nothing left to decide

Certified with Sandals and the Hyatt Inclusive Collection. The appeal of an all-inclusive is that you stop making decisions when you land — which only works if the right decisions got made beforehand.

All-inclusive resorts are the easiest kind of trip to book badly. They photograph identically, the brochures use the same eleven adjectives, and the difference between a resort you love and one you tolerate is almost never visible on the website.

What I'm actually matching you to

The right kind of quiet — or the right kind of loud

Some all-inclusives are adults-only and genuinely serene. Some are adults-only and have a foam party at three in the afternoon. Some are family resorts where the kids' club is excellent and the adult pool is a real refuge, and some are family resorts where there is nowhere to escape. These are not small differences, and no resort describes itself as the second one.

The room category, which is the whole ball game

At most all-inclusives the room tier doesn't just change your bed — it changes your resort. Swim-up access, a butler, a separate à-la-carte reservation pool, a private beach section, access to a quieter club lounge. At Sandals and the Hyatt Inclusive properties in particular, moving up one or two categories often changes the trip more than moving to a different resort would.

It also works in reverse. I'll tell you when the premium tier is a nice room and nothing more, and you should put that money into a longer stay or a better flight.

Food, honestly assessed

"Ten restaurants" means very little if six require reservations that are gone by nine in the morning, or if the good one is a supplement. I'd rather send you to a resort with four genuinely good restaurants and no reservation system than one with twelve and a queue.

The perks, specifically

Through Signature Travel Network I can attach benefits to many resort bookings that aren't available when you book direct or through a discount site. Depending on the property and the rate, that looks like a resort credit to spend on spa or dining, a room upgrade where inventory allows, daily breakfast at properties where it isn't already included, or early check-in and late checkout.

These vary by property and by date. I'll tell you what's available on your specific booking rather than listing what's theoretically possible — and if there's nothing worth having, I'll say that instead.

On price

Same published rate, no markup. On the great majority of resort bookings there's no planning fee — I'm paid by the resort after you travel. Where a trip is complex enough to warrant a fee, you'll know the number up front, before you're committed to anything.

Honeymoons and celebrations

All-inclusives are where most honeymoons land, and there's a real difference between a resort that notes "honeymoon" on your booking and one that does something about it. Sandals and the Hyatt Inclusive properties both run genuine celebration programmes — room upgrades subject to availability, a bottle on arrival, a private dinner — but most of it has to be arranged in advance by whoever made the booking. Turning up and mentioning it at check-in gets you a card on the pillow.

When to book

Three to six months ahead covers most of the year. Push earlier for the Caribbean between Christmas and Easter, for anything over a US holiday weekend, and for the small adults-only properties that only have sixty rooms to begin with. Hurricane season — roughly June to November — brings real savings and real risk; whether that trade makes sense depends on how much flexibility you have and how you feel about travel insurance, which we'll talk about.
