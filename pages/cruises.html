Cruises

Cruise planning from someone who has actually sailed

Ocean, river, and expedition. I'm a CLIA Certified Cruise Counsellor with line certifications across Virgin Voyages, Royal Caribbean, and Carnival — and the reason that matters is not the badge.

Most people who come away disappointed by a cruise did not pick the wrong destination. They picked the wrong ship, or the wrong cabin on the right ship, and nobody told them the difference before they paid.

The three decisions that actually matter

Itinerary is the fun part, and it's the part every booking site leads with. It's also the least likely thing to ruin your week. What ruins a cruise is a ship whose culture doesn't match yours, a cabin under the pool deck, or a sailing date you booked without knowing what the weather does there in October.

1. The ship, not the line

Cruise lines are not monoliths. Two ships flying the same flag can feel like entirely different holidays — one built around a waterpark and a buffet that seats four thousand, the other around adult-only sun decks and a restaurant you need to book. Royal Caribbean's newest hardware is a different proposition from its older, smaller ships. Virgin is adults-only and leans into it. Carnival's fleet spans a genuinely wide range.

I'll ask you what a good day at sea looks like before I ask you where you want to go, because that answer narrows the fleet faster than a destination ever will.

2. The cabin category

The gap between the cheapest inside cabin and a mid-tier balcony is usually smaller than people expect, and the difference it makes to a seven-night sailing is not. But it works the other way too — I've talked plenty of people out of a suite they didn't need on an itinerary where they'd barely be in the room.

What I'm really watching for is location. Deck placement, what's directly above and below you, how far you are from the lifts, whether that "obstructed view" is a rope or an entire lifeboat. This is the part that's invisible on a booking site and obvious to anyone who has sailed the ship.

3. When you sail

Alaska in May is a different trip from Alaska in August, and both are cheaper and quieter than Alaska in July. Caribbean hurricane season carries real risk and real savings, and whether that trade is worth it depends entirely on how much flexibility you have. Repositioning sailings are extraordinary value if you have the time and don't mind sea days.

What I book

  • Ocean cruising — Caribbean, Alaska, Mexico, Mediterranean, transatlantic and repositioning sailings.
  • River cruising — smaller ships, no sea days, and a different pace entirely. Genuinely worth considering if the idea of a four-thousand-passenger ship puts you off.
  • Expedition — smaller vessels going where the big ones can't, with naturalists aboard rather than production shows.
  • Family sailings — including Disney, where I hold graduate certification, and the lines with the kids' clubs that are actually good.
  • Group and celebration sailings — milestone birthdays, family reunions, anniversary trips where you need several cabins near each other.

What you get that you can't get yourself

I book the same published fare you'd find online. What I add on top comes through Signature Travel Network, and on many sailings that means onboard credit, prepaid gratuities, or a shore-excursion credit attached to your booking before you sail. It's not available on every ship and every date, and I'll tell you plainly when there's nothing to add rather than pretend otherwise.

I also watch your booking after it's made. Cruise fares move. If the price drops before final payment and the fare code allows it, I'll rebook you — which is a thing that only happens if someone is actually looking.

On price

You will not pay more for booking through me. On the overwhelming majority of cruise bookings there's no planning fee at all — I'm paid a commission by the cruise line after you sail, out of their margin, not added to your fare. If a trip is ever complex enough to need a fee, you'll hear the number before you commit to anything.

When to book

Six to twelve months out is the sweet spot for most sailings — enough lead time for cabin choice, close enough that the schedule is firm. Push that further for Alaska, for holiday sailings, and for anything over Christmas and New Year, where the good cabins are gone a year ahead. Last-minute deals exist, but they're deals on whatever nobody else wanted.
