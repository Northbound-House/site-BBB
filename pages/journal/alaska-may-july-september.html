Alaska: May vs. July vs. September

Three very different vacations sold under one name, at three very different prices. Picking the month matters more than picking the ship.

The Alaska cruise season runs roughly from late April to late September, and people tend to treat that as one product with a price curve. It isn't. The month you sail changes the weather, the wildlife, the daylight, what's open on shore, and what you pay — often by more than the difference between two cabin categories.

May — the quiet, cheap, surprisingly dry one

May is the most underrated month in Alaska and the one I recommend most often to people who care about value.

What's good: it's the driest part of the season in Southeast Alaska. Prices are at their lowest outside the very start and end. Snow is still on the peaks, which makes the scenic cruising look the way people imagine Alaska looking. Ports are noticeably less crowded — a small town handling two ships instead of five is a different place.

What's not: it's cold, genuinely. Some shore operators haven't opened for the season, particularly the smaller wildlife and fishing outfits. Bear viewing is poor to nonexistent early in the month. Daylight is long but not yet at its peak.

Book May if: your priority is scenery, photography, and paying less. Bring warm layers and don't count on every excursion running.

July — the warm, busy, expensive one

July is peak, and it's peak for good reasons.

What's good: the warmest temperatures of the season, and the longest usable daylight. Every excursion operator is running. Salmon runs bring the bears out, so wildlife viewing is at its most reliable. If you're traveling with children, or with anyone who will be miserable if it's cold, this is the safe choice.

What's not: the highest prices of the year, and the ports are at their busiest. Popular excursions — the glacier flightseeing, the better whale-watching boats — sell out months ahead. It also rains; July is warmer than May but not drier.

Book July if: you want maximum wildlife and maximum comfort, and you're prepared to book early and pay for it.

September — the gold, moody, gambling one

September is the month I'd pick for myself, with a caveat.

What's good: fall color on the hillsides, which almost nobody expects from Alaska. Prices drop sharply after Labor Day. Crowds thin out. Wildlife is active and feeding ahead of winter. And once the nights get properly dark again, there's a real chance of aurora on a clear night — which is not a thing you get in July, because it never gets dark enough.

What's not: it's the wettest stretch of the season, and it gets wetter as the month goes on. Operators start closing down in the last two weeks. Daylight shortens fast. You are, to a degree, gambling on the weather.

Book September if: you'll take a moodier, grayer, emptier Alaska in exchange for color, aurora odds, and a much lower fare — and you won't be devastated by three wet days.

The short version

  • Value and scenery: May.
  • Wildlife, warmth, and families: July.
  • Color, aurora odds, and fewer people: September.

Two things that matter more than the month

Which side of the ship you're on. On a round-trip Inside Passage sailing you'll see scenery from both sides at different points, so it evens out. On a one-way Gulf of Alaska sailing it doesn't even out nearly as much. This is a real conversation to have before you pick a cabin, not after.

Whether the itinerary includes Glacier Bay. Access is limited by permit, so not every ship goes, and it's the single most requested day of the trip. If it matters to you, it needs checking on the specific sailing rather than assuming the line covers it.

When to book

Earlier than most itineraries. Alaska has a short season and finite capacity, and the good cabins on the good sailings go a long way out. Twelve months ahead is normal; six is late for July.
