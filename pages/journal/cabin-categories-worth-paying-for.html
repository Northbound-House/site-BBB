The cabin categories worth paying for — and the ones that aren't

An honest accounting, including the times the cheaper cabin was the better call, and the upgrade that matters more than any of them.

Every cruise line sells four broad things: an inside cabin, an oceanview, a balcony, and a suite. The gaps between them are priced as though each is obviously better than the last. They aren't, and which one is right for you depends far more on the itinerary than on your budget.

Inside cabins

Cheapest, no window, and better than their reputation. On a port-intensive itinerary where you're ashore from eight until five and back on board only to eat and sleep, an inside cabin is a perfectly rational choice — you're buying a dark, quiet room to sleep in, and inside cabins are genuinely the darkest and often the quietest on the ship.

Where they fail is on sea days and on scenic sailings. If you'll spend real daylight hours in the room, the absence of natural light stops being a bargain and starts being oppressive.

Oceanview

The category I steer people away from most often. You get a window that doesn't open, usually on a lower deck, frequently for a price much closer to a balcony than to an inside.

There are two good reasons to book one: you want daylight but get seasick and prefer being low and central, or the balcony price on that sailing is genuinely silly. Otherwise, look at the gap between oceanview and balcony before you assume it's the sensible middle.

Balconies

The upgrade that most often earns its money — on the right itinerary.

Worth it on: Alaska, Norway, the fjords, transatlantic and repositioning sailings, anything with several sea days, and any itinerary where the scenery happens while you're moving. On an Alaska sailing a balcony isn't a luxury; it's where you'll spend the trip.

Often not worth it on: a short, warm, port-heavy Caribbean run where you're off the ship every day and the deck is where you want to be anyway. Plenty of people pay a large premium for a balcony they use twice.

The honest test: picture the daylight hours you'll be aboard and moving. If that's a lot of hours, book the balcony. If it's almost none, put the money into a better flight or an extra night pre-cruise.

Suites

Suites are two different products sold under one word, and it matters which one you're buying.

A suite that is a bigger room is a nice room. If space is what you want, fine — but it's a room.

A suite that comes with a separate restaurant, a concierge, priority tender and embarkation, and a dedicated sun deck is a different vacation. On the big ships in particular, that access changes your day: you skip the line that defines everyone else's morning. That's worth real money to some people and nothing at all to others.

Before you book one, get someone to tell you plainly which of the two you're being sold.

The upgrade that beats all of them

Deck placement. A mid-ship cabin on a lower-priced category will out-perform a badly placed cabin two tiers up, every single time.

What I'm checking: what's directly above and below you (pool deck, buffet, theatre and the crew areas are the usual offenders), how far you are from the elevators, whether the deck below is a nightclub, and whether an "obstructed view" means a rope or an entire lifeboat. None of it appears on a booking site. All of it is knowable from the deck plan and from having sailed the ship.

Three specifics that catch people out

Connecting cabins. If you're traveling with family, connecting rooms are limited, fixed in location, and go early. This is the most common thing I have to solve at short notice and the one with the fewest options once inventory is gone.

Guarantee rates. Booking a “guarantee” buys you a category, not a cabin — the line assigns it, sometimes days before sailing, sometimes with a free bump upward. It's a real saving and a real gamble. Don't take one if location matters to you, and never take one if you need connecting rooms.

Aft and forward. Aft cabins often have oversized balconies and a wake view, and more movement. Forward cabins get the most motion of all. If anyone in the party is prone to seasickness, mid-ship and low is the answer, whatever the category.

So what would I book you?

Scenic itinerary, sea days, Alaska or northern Europe: balcony, mid-ship, and I'd rather drop a category to get the placement right than move up one and be under the pool deck.

Warm, port-heavy, seven nights: often the inside or a low balcony, with the difference spent on the parts of the trip you'll actually remember.

Big ship, and you hate queuing: this is the case where the suite tier genuinely earns it — but for the access, not the square footage.
