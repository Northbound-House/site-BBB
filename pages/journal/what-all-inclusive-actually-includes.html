What “all-inclusive” actually includes

Meals and drinks, obviously. But premium spirits, the good restaurants, the spa, the transfers and the wifi are where it gets interesting.

“All-inclusive” is a marketing category, not a standard. Nobody audits it. Two resorts on the same beach can use the identical phrase and mean noticeably different things, and the difference only becomes obvious on the last morning when someone hands you a folio.

Here's the honest breakdown.

Almost always included

  • All meals, including buffet and at least some of the à-la-carte restaurants.
  • Standard drinks — house wine and beer, well spirits, soft drinks, coffee.
  • Non-motorized watersports — kayaks, paddleboards, snorkel gear, sometimes small sailboats.
  • Room service, though hours vary a lot and some tiers get it and others don't.
  • Tips, at most Caribbean and Mexican properties. Not universally.
  • Kids' club access at family resorts, during posted hours.

Usually extra, and this is where the bill grows

  • Premium and top-shelf spirits. The single most common surprise. "Unlimited drinks" often means unlimited house drinks, and the brand you actually order sits one tier up.
  • Specialty restaurants beyond your allowance. Some properties include everything; some give you a set number of à-la-carte dinners per stay based on your room tier; some charge per visit.
  • The spa. Treatments are essentially never included. The thermal circuit sometimes is.
  • Motorized watersports — jet skis, parasailing, diving beyond an intro pool session.
  • Excursions, always.
  • Wifi above the basic tier. Basic is usually free now; the tier that streams often isn't.
  • Airport transfers. Included at some brands as standard, a paid add-on at others. Worth checking, because it's a meaningful number in places where the airport is ninety minutes away.
  • Romantic dinners on the beach, the private cabana, the photographer. All of it priced separately, all of it pitched to you on arrival day.

The room tier is the real variable

At most all-inclusives, moving up a room category doesn't just change your bed. It changes which resort you're staying at.

Higher tiers commonly unlock a separate reservation pool for the good restaurants, a quieter section of beach, a club lounge with its own bar, butler service, and — critically — a different booking window for the things that sell out. At the Sandals and Hyatt Inclusive properties I work with most, moving up one or two categories often changes the trip more than moving to a different resort would.

It works in reverse too. Plenty of premium tiers are a nicer room and nothing else, and that money is better spent on two extra nights.

Four questions to ask before you book

  1. Which spirits are in the included tier, and what does the next tier up cost?
  2. How many à-la-carte dinners does my room category include, and when can I reserve them?
  3. Are airport transfers included, and how long is the drive?
  4. What does the room tier unlock beyond the room itself?

“Ten restaurants” is a number, not a promise

A resort advertising ten restaurants may have four you can walk into, four that need a reservation released at 9am daily, and two carrying a supplement. That's still a good resort — but it's a resort you need to arrive at with a plan, and nobody tells you that at booking.

I'd rather send you somewhere with four good restaurants and no reservation system than twelve and a waitlist. Which one you're getting is knowable in advance, and it's the sort of thing worth asking about.

What this means for choosing

Compare the all-in number, not the nightly rate. Take the fare, add the drinks tier you'll realistically want, the transfers if they're extra, and one spa treatment if that's part of why you're going. Do that for both shortlisted resorts and the ranking frequently flips.
