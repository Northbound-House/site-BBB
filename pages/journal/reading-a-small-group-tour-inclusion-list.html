How to read a small group tour's inclusion list

Where the optional excursions are the actual highlights, the headline price is fiction. Eight things to check before you book.

Tour itineraries are written to be skimmed. The day-by-day reads beautifully, the photos are excellent, and the price looks competitive against the operator next to it. Then you get home and the trip cost forty percent more than the number you compared.

None of that is fraud. It's just that inclusion lists are written by marketing departments, and there are eight places where the meaning is doing a lot of work.

1. The group cap, and whether it's a cap

“Small group” is completely unregulated. I've seen it used at thirty-eight people. What you want is a stated maximum — “maximum 16” — not an average, and not “typically around…”. An average of eighteen means some departures run at twenty-six.

2. The single supplement

This is where the real money hides if you're traveling alone. Supplements range from waived on selected departures, through a room-share matching scheme, to eighty percent on top of the fare. It is frequently the single largest variable between two otherwise similar tours, and it's usually in the small print rather than the price box.

3. Which meals, and how many

Look for the count, not the adjective. “Most meals included” is not a number. A fourteen-day tour listing “13 breakfasts, 4 lunches, 3 dinners” is asking you to fund about twenty meals in restaurants the operator has not chosen — which is fine if you like that, and a real cost if you didn't budget for it.

4. Optional excursions that are the actual highlights

This is the big one. If the itinerary lists the hot-air balloon, the tomb, the private after-hours museum entry and the cooking class as optional extras, then the headline price buys you the bus and the hotel. Add up every optional you'd genuinely be sad to miss, put that on the fare, and only then compare operators.

5. Entrance fees and internal flights

Two separate line items that behave the same way. An itinerary can look inclusive and still leave you paying at every gate. Internal flights are worse because they're large and non-optional — if the tour crosses a country in a way that needs a flight, and the flight isn't in the fare, that's a several-hundred-dollar gap in the comparison.

6. Tipping, and the word “kitty”

Some operators include all gratuities. Some ask for a per-person, per-day tipping kitty collected on day one, which can run to a few hundred dollars over a long tour. Neither is wrong; only one of them is in the price you compared.

7. Hotel descriptions that mean nothing

“First-class,” “superior tourist,” and “carefully selected” are not classifications. They're adjectives. What you want is named properties, or at minimum “4-star or similar” with examples. If an operator won't name hotels until final documents, that tells you something.

8. Who employs the guide

The least visible item on the list and the best predictor of quality. Operators who employ their guides directly, year-round, get consistency. Operators who subcontract per departure get whoever was available. It's rarely stated outright, but it's a fair question to ask, and the answer usually arrives quickly from the good ones.

The comparison that actually works

Take both shortlisted tours. To each fare, add: the single supplement if it applies, every optional you'd actually do, an estimate for the uncovered meals, internal flights, entrance fees, and the tipping kitty. Then compare.

In my experience the cheaper headline loses about half the time, and the gap can be substantial.

Two green flags

Genuine free time. An itinerary with gaps in it was designed by someone who has been on a tour. Eleven hours of programming a day was designed by someone selling one.

No mandatory shopping stops. Still more common than it should be, particularly on lower-priced tours in some regions. A good operator will tell you plainly that there aren't any.

Chad and I live on the road full-time, so I have opinions about logistics that most people are lucky enough never to need. A good operator absorbs all of it — and the inclusion list is where you find out whether this one does.
